The Twelve-Dollar Mystery

A support team runs an answer-drafting workload. Each customer ticket triggers a RAG retrieval, an LLM generation pass, and a quality check. The system handles roughly 1,000 tickets per day, and the team is evaluating whether to stay on their current provider or switch to a cheaper alternative.

The comparison looks straightforward. Route A charges $3.00 per million input tokens and $15.00 per million output tokens. Route B charges $1.80 per million input tokens and $8.00 per million output tokens. Route B is 40% cheaper on input and 47% cheaper on output. The procurement spreadsheet says: switch to Route B, save $4,200 per month.

The team switches. Thirty days later, the inference bill is higher than before. The support burden has increased. The product manager is asking what happened.

This chapter is an autopsy of a single day on the new route. It will show what the token-price comparison missed, and why.

What The Token Price Missed

1. Cache hit rate was worse than expected

The team designed the prompt with a stable system prompt and tool definitions at the top—roughly 1,800 tokens of cache-eligible prefix. On Route A, 60% of requests hit the cache. On Route B, the hit rate dropped to 35%.

Why? Three reasons the team did not anticipate:

  • Route B uses a different cache implementation with a shorter effective TTL. Requests arriving more than a few minutes apart saw cold cache.

  • The retrieval context changes per ticket. When retrieval context sits inside the cache-eligible prefix, every new document set breaks the prefix match.

  • Route B’s load balancer distributes across more replicas. Cache is per-replica. Fewer requests land on the same replica within the TTL window.

The pricing page said cache reads were 50% cheaper than uncached input. That discount matters only when cache hits actually happen. At 35% hit rate instead of 60%, the effective input cost is higher than the spreadsheet assumed.

2. Output tokens were longer

Route B’s model produced longer outputs on average. Requests 4, 6, and 8 show output lengths of 510, 380, and 620 tokens—well above the 250-token median the team modeled. Output tokens are the expensive side of the bill. On many major API providers, output costs 2-6x more than input per token. A 40% increase in average output length can erase a 47% discount on the per-token output price.

This is not a bug. Different models have different verbosity profiles, and the support prompt was tuned for Route A’s model. Prompt portability is not free.

3. Retries and repairs are invisible on the pricing page

Two of eight first attempts failed their SLO (requests 4 and 8). Both were retried. One first attempt failed its quality gate (request 3) and was repaired with a corrected retrieval context. The eval grader call (request 11) checked the failed outputs.

These four additional requests—retries, eval, repair—are not in the pricing comparison. They are in the trace. Each one consumed input tokens, output tokens, compute time, and latency budget. The inference cost of the four recovery requests was roughly 30% of the total inference bill for those twelve requests.

4. Quality failures have downstream costs

Request 3 failed the quality gate. Its answer missed a key constraint from the customer’s account. The repair call (request 12) fixed it, but the customer waited an extra 3.2 seconds. If the repair had also failed, the ticket would have escalated to a human agent.

On this workload, 5% of tickets escalate to humans. Each human escalation costs roughly $2 in agent time. On 1,000 tickets per day, 50 escalations cost $100. The daily inference bill is about $14. Human escalation costs 7x the inference bill.

The pricing page does not have a column for human escalation cost per failed answer.

5. The SLO gate changes the denominator

The team’s SLO says: TTFT under 800ms, E2E under 5 seconds, eval score above 0.85. Of the eight first attempts, five passed all three gates. Three failed at least one gate. Two were recovered by retries; one was recovered by repair.

The denominator is not “requests sent to the provider.” It is “accepted answers that passed quality, latency, and compliance gates.” Eight requests produced eight answers. Five were accepted on the first try. Three needed recovery. The inference cost per accepted answer is higher than the cost per request.

What Route A Was Hiding

Here is the part the team did not expect. Route A was more expensive per token— but it had a 60% cache hit rate, shorter average outputs, a 91% first-attempt quality pass rate, and fewer retries. The loaded cost per accepted answer on Route A was $0.135. Route B, at $0.172, was 27% more expensive per accepted answer despite being 40% cheaper per input token.

The spreadsheet picked the wrong route.

Why This Happens

This is not a story about one bad migration. It is a pattern. It happens whenever a team compares inference options using token price alone:

The numerator problem. Token price captures only the inference provider bill. It misses eval cost, retry cost, repair cost, human escalation, operational overhead, and engineering time. On workloads where quality is variable, the non-token costs can dominate.

The denominator problem. Dividing by total requests, total tokens, or total tickets conflates attempted work with accepted work. If 18% of attempts fail quality, latency, or compliance gates, the cost per accepted unit is 22% higher than the cost per attempt.

The cache problem. Prompt caching economics depend on prefix stability, TTL, inter-request timing, and routing topology. A cache discount on the pricing page is not a cache discount in production unless the cache hits.

The output-length problem. Different models produce different output distributions for the same prompt. Output tokens are more expensive than input tokens. A model that produces 40% more output can cost more despite a lower per-token price.

The quality problem. A model with a lower first-attempt pass rate generates more retries, repairs, and human escalations. Each retry is an inference call that does not appear in the pricing comparison. Each escalation is a cost that does not appear on the inference bill at all.

The Book’s Promise

This manual teaches one skill: how to measure, model, and operate production inference decisions so you can choose the cheapest reliable architecture that still meets quality, latency, reliability, and customer requirements.

The economic unit is not the token. It is not the request. It is not the GPU-hour. It is the accepted work unit: one successful task completion that passes your quality gate, meets your latency SLO, complies with your data constraints, and is worth paying for.

Every chapter in this book follows the same rhythm:

  1. A wrong conclusion that a competent team might reach from a naive view.

  2. The mechanism that makes the conclusion wrong.

  3. A trace or worked example that shows the gap.

  4. A formula with units.

  5. A decision rule with caveats.

  6. A calculator view or artifact the reader can use in a meeting.

  7. The conditions under which the rule breaks.

If you already have clean traces, good evals, and invoice reconciliation, start at Part 2 (Serving Physics) or Part 3 (Workload Economics). If you do not yet have these, start with Part 1 (The Economic Unit) and build them.

The next chapter defines the loaded cost per result—LCPR-2026—and explains why every number in this book is normalized to accepted output, not raw tokens.
